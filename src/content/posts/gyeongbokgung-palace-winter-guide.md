If you only see one historic site in Seoul, make it **Gyeongbokgung Palace** (경복궁). Built in 1395 as the main royal palace of the Joseon dynasty, it sits at the top of Sejong-daero with the granite peak of Bugaksan rising directly behind it — the city's founders picked the spot for exactly that view. Today it's a huge walled complex of throne halls, courtyards, pavilions and ponds, right in the middle of downtown, and a ticket costs about the same as a cup of convenience-store coffee.

Winter is an underrated time to go. The tour-bus crowds thin out, the low sun brings out the colors of the painted eaves, and after a snowfall the palace looks like an ink painting. The catch is the short day: in winter the gates close at 5pm and **last entry is 4pm**, so this is a morning or early-afternoon plan.

> **Heads up:** The photos below show Gyeongbokgung in different seasons and years, not this week. The snow scenes are from past winters — snow in Seoul is never guaranteed.

## The royal guard changing ceremony

  <img src="/images/posts/gyeongbokgung-palace-winter-guide/01.jpg" alt="Guards in red Joseon-era uniforms standing in a line in front of Heungnyemun Gate on a sunny day" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 1 · Guards lined up in front of Heungnyemun, the second gate inside Gwanghwamun.</figcaption>
</figure>

Time your arrival for the **Royal Guard Changing Ceremony**, a re-enactment of the Joseon-era gate watch with drums, banners and guards in bright red and blue uniforms. It usually runs at **10am and 2pm** for about 20 minutes in the courtyard between Gwanghwamun and Heungnyemun gates. It's free, it happens outside the ticket gate, and you don't need to book — just arrive 10–15 minutes early to get a spot at the front. There's no ceremony on Tuesdays (the palace is closed), and it can be cancelled in heavy rain or extreme weather.

## Geunjeongjeon, the throne hall

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/gyeongbokgung-palace-winter-guide/03.jpg" alt="The two-story Geunjeongjeon throne hall rising above a stone terrace crowded with visitors" loading="lazy" />

Once you're through the ticket gate, the first big moment is **Geunjeongjeon** (근정전), the throne hall where kings received officials and foreign envoys and held coronations. It sits on a two-tier stone platform above a wide courtyard paved with rough granite slabs, with rows of stone markers showing where officials stood by rank. It's much bigger in person than it looks in photos, and you can peer inside at the throne and the painted screen of the sun, moon and five peaks behind it.

## The quieter inner courts

Walk north past the king's office (Sajeongjeon) and the royal living quarters and the crowds drop off fast. The buildings get smaller and more domestic, the courtyards narrower, and the stone walls start framing Bugaksan behind them. Early morning is the best time for this part — you'll often have whole courtyards to yourself.

## Gyeonghoeru and Hyangwonjeong

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/gyeongbokgung-palace-winter-guide/08.jpg" alt="Gyeonghoeru Pavilion standing on stone pillars over a still pond, reflected in the water" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 8 · Gyeonghoeru Pavilion and its reflection.</figcaption>
</figure>

On the west side, **Gyeonghoeru** (경회루) is a large pavilion raised on 48 stone pillars over a square pond — the place where kings hosted banquets for foreign envoys. On a calm day the pond turns into a mirror, and walking slowly around the edge gives you a different angle every few steps.

Further north, **Hyangwonjeong** (향원정) is a small hexagonal pavilion on an island in its own pond, reached by a wooden bridge. It's the shot that turns up on every "Seoul in winter" list when there's snow.

## Two free museums on the grounds

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/gyeongbokgung-palace-winter-guide/13.jpg" alt="The tiered pagoda-style tower of the National Folk Museum of Korea surrounded by snow and pine trees" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 13 · The National Folk Museum building in winter.</figcaption>
</figure>

Two national museums sit right next to the palace and both are free: the **National Folk Museum of Korea**, in the northeast corner (look for the pagoda-style tower), covers everyday life in traditional Korea, and the **National Palace Museum of Korea**, near the west side, displays royal treasures from the Joseon court. Both are heated, which matters on a sub-zero January afternoon. Check the official sites for their current hours, as they differ from the palace's.

## Tickets, prices and free entry

- **Adults (19–64):** ₩3,000 (about US$2). Buy at the ticket office or the machines in front of Heungnyemun; the machines have an English menu and take cards.
- **Free for foreign visitors aged 18 and under or 65 and over.** You may read that people 24 and under get in free — that rule is for Korean nationals only.
- **Free if you're wearing hanbok.** It needs to be a proper top and skirt or trousers; rental outfits from the shops around the palace count.
- **Free on Culture Day**, the last Wednesday of each month (the next one is December 30, 2026).
- **Integrated palace pass:** ₩6,000 covers Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung and Jongmyo Shrine. It doesn't include Changdeokgung's Secret Garden. Check the terms at the ticket office.

Korea's heritage agency has been discussing higher palace admission fees for 2027, but no new prices had been announced as of late November 2026 — check the official site if you're visiting next year.

## How to get there

The palace is at 161 Sajik-ro, Jongno-gu.

- **Gyeongbokgung Station (Line 3), Exit 5** is the closest. An underground passage brings you up on the palace grounds near the National Palace Museum, a short walk from the ticket office.
- **Gwanghwamun Station (Line 5), Exit 2** is about a 10-minute walk north and lets you enter the proper way, through the main Gwanghwamun Gate.
- **From Myeongdong:** Line 4 one stop to Chungmuro, then Line 3 to Gyeongbokgung — roughly 20 minutes.
- **From Hongdae:** Line 2 to Euljiro 3-ga, then Line 3 to Gyeongbokgung — roughly 30–35 minutes.

For walking directions, use **Naver Map** or **KakaoMap** and search 경복궁 (or "Gyeongbokgung"). Google Maps will show subway routes in Korea but won't give you turn-by-turn walking directions.

## Tips for international visitors

- **Mind the Tuesday closure.** It catches a lot of people out. If Tuesday is your only free day, Deoksugung (closed Mondays) is the backup.
- **Go early.** Arrive for the 9am opening, catch the 10am ceremony on your way in, and you'll have the inner courts mostly to yourself. Allow at least two hours inside.
- **Dress for the cold.** The courtyards are open stone with no shade or windbreak, and Seoul winters regularly drop below freezing. Hot packs from any convenience store help.
- **Hanbok in winter:** many rental shops nearby offer padded or fur-trimmed outfits and outer layers. Booking ahead online is easiest; staff in the bigger shops usually speak some English.
- **Free English tour:** guided walks leave from the information center just inside Heungnyemun, usually at 11am, 1:30pm and once mid-afternoon (check the board for the day's times). No booking needed for small groups.
- **Combine it with:** the hanok alleys of [Bukchon Hanok Village](/posts/bukchon-hanok-village-guide/), a 15-minute walk east; the [Seoul Lantern Festival](/posts/seoul-lantern-festival-2026/) on Cheonggyecheon Stream from December 11; or a late lunch at [Gwangjang Market](/posts/gwangjang-market-food-guide/).

## Is it worth it?

Absolutely. For ₩3,000 — or nothing at all if you rent hanbok — you get Seoul's most important historic site, a free piece of living theater at the gate and two free museums next door. Just plan around the short winter day and the Tuesday closure, and if it snows during your trip, drop everything and go the next morning.
