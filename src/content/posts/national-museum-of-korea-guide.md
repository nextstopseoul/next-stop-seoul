When Seoul turns cold and grey, the **National Museum of Korea** is one of the best ways to spend half a day without spending a won. The permanent galleries are free, the building is enormous and heated, and the subway drops you off almost at the door through an underground walkway — you barely have to step outside.

This isn't a dusty, niche museum, either. It now ranks among the busiest museums in the world, drawing more than six million visitors a year, and a big part of that is the K-culture wave: travelers who came for K-pop and dramas are increasingly heading here to see where Korean design and history actually come from. Think of it as the "greatest hits" of Korea's past, from prehistoric tools to Silla gold and Joseon porcelain, all under one roof in Yongsan.

There's also fresh news for this winter: from **November 27, 2026 to March 21, 2027**, the museum hosts a special exhibition, *War, Art and Life*, organized with Switzerland's Kunsthaus Zürich. It brings around 90 works, including paintings by the Swiss artist Ferdinand Hodler. Special exhibitions charge admission, so check the museum's website for ticket prices.

> **Heads up:** The photos in this guide show the museum's permanent galleries on ordinary days, not this winter's special exhibition.

## The Room of Quiet Contemplation

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/national-museum-of-korea-guide/01.jpg" alt="Two pensive bodhisattva statues seen from behind, facing each other across a dark room with a starry ceiling and glowing curved walls" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 1 · The Room of Quiet Contemplation on the second floor.</figcaption>
</figure>

If you only see one thing, make it this. The **Room of Quiet Contemplation** (사유의 방, *sayu-ui bang*) on the second floor was built for just two objects: a pair of gilt-bronze **Pensive Bodhisattva** statues, both designated National Treasures. One dates to the late 6th century and the other to the early 7th.

You reach them through a long, dark corridor. The room itself is designed to slow you down: the floor rises gently and the walls slant, the clay-coloured walls glow softly, and the ceiling is dotted with tiny lights like a night sky. It's meant to be quiet, so keep your voice low — even guided groups do.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/national-museum-of-korea-guide/02.jpg" alt="Front view of two gilt-bronze pensive bodhisattvas on low pedestals under a starry ceiling" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 2 · The two National Treasure bodhisattvas, displayed side by side.</figcaption>
</figure>

Walk all the way around them. Each figure sits with one leg crossed over the other and fingertips resting lightly on the cheek, eyes half closed, with the faintest smile. Up close, you can see why the statues have become a symbol of the museum — and why the miniature replicas are some of the best-selling souvenirs in the museum shop.

## Gold from Korea's ancient kingdoms

The first-floor history galleries are where the museum really shows off. Korea's ancient kingdoms — Silla especially — were famous for goldwork, and the craftsmanship here is astonishing.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/national-museum-of-korea-guide/05.jpg" alt="Detail of an ancient gold crown with tall flame- and tree-shaped ornaments and small gold flowers" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 5 · An ancient gold crown with flame-like uprights.</figcaption>
</figure>

Look for the crowns with their tall tree- and antler-like uprights, made from thin, hammered sheet gold. They were made to shimmer and tremble when worn.

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/national-museum-of-korea-guide/06.jpg" alt="A pair of ancient gold earrings with thick rings and long chains of tiny dangling gold leaves" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 6 · Gold earrings covered in tiny dangling spangles.</figcaption>
</figure>

The jewelry is just as detailed: earrings dripping with dozens of tiny gold leaves, and a ceremonial belt hung with long pendants. Pieces like these were buried with royalty and elites, and they're a big reason Gyeongju, the old Silla capital, is called a "museum without walls."

Part of the fun is spotting the quirky pieces between the headline exhibits.

Upstairs on the third floor, the Celadon Room and the Buncheong and White Porcelain Room are both open — good for seeing the jade-green Goryeo celadon and the plain, elegant white wares of the Joseon period. Before you leave, walk to the far end of the main hall to see the towering ten-story stone pagoda standing indoors.

> **Heads up:** Several upper-floor rooms are closed for renovation until **January 28, 2027**: the Buddhist Sculpture and Metal Craft rooms on the third floor, and the Buddhist Painting, Lacquerware and some Calligraphy & Painting rooms on the second floor. The Room of Quiet Contemplation is not on the closure list, but check the official site before you go if it's your main reason for visiting.

## How to get there

- **Subway:** Take **Line 4** or the **Gyeongui-Jungang Line** to **Ichon Station** (이촌역). Follow the museum signs from **Exit 2** through the underground walkway; it's about a 5-minute walk.
- **From Myeongdong:** Line 4 runs directly from Myeongdong Station to Ichon, roughly 15 minutes with no transfer.
- **From Hongdae:** The Gyeongui-Jungang Line runs directly from Hongik University Station to Ichon in about 20 minutes, but trains are less frequent than on other lines. Line 2 to Line 4 is a backup.
- **Maps:** Search **국립중앙박물관** in Naver Map or KakaoMap. Google Maps shows transit routes in Korea but doesn't give turn-by-turn walking directions, so the Korean apps are more reliable for the final stretch.

## Tips for international visitors

- **Go on a Wednesday or Saturday evening.** The museum stays open until 9pm on those days (last entry 8:30pm), and the crowds thin out after dinner. Weekends and school holidays can mean queues at the entrance.
- **Mind the closures.** Besides January 1, Lunar New Year's Day and Chuseok, the galleries close on the first Monday of March, June, September and December — so the museum is **closed on Monday, December 7, 2026**.
- **Free — for now.** The permanent galleries are free, but Korea is preparing to charge admission, reportedly from 2027. If you're visiting next year, check the official site for fees.
- **Get an audio guide.** Gallery labels have English, and audio guides are offered in several languages, including English — ask at the information desk.
- **Pick your rooms in advance.** The building is long, and trying to see everything is exhausting. A good half-day plan: Room of Quiet Contemplation, the ancient history galleries on the first floor, then the porcelain rooms on the third floor.
- **Save time for the shop.** The museum's own MU:DS souvenirs, especially the mini pensive bodhisattvas and moon-jar items, are hugely popular with locals.
- **Pair it with:** a walk in the museum's outdoor garden and pond (open 7am–10pm), the Yongsan Family Park next door, or a market dinner in Jongno — see our [Gwangjang Market food guide](/posts/gwangjang-market-food-guide/). On a rainy day, [Seoul Botanic Park's winter greenhouse](/posts/seoul-botanic-park-winter/) is another warm indoor option.

## Is it worth it?

Absolutely, especially in winter. It's free, easy to reach, warm and genuinely world-class. The Room of Quiet Contemplation alone is worth the subway ride, and if you add the *War, Art and Life* exhibition after November 27, you can easily fill a whole day.
