Tiled roofs stepping down a hillside, wooden gates, stone walls — and, on a clear day, N Seoul Tower floating above it all. **Bukchon Hanok Village** (북촌한옥마을) is the picture of "old Seoul" that ends up on almost every first-timer's itinerary, and it costs nothing to walk around. Winter is a lovely time to go: the trees are bare, so the rooflines show more clearly, the light is low and golden, and the crowds are thinner than in spring and autumn.

But Bukchon is not a theme park. It's a lived-in neighborhood squeezed between two royal palaces, **Gyeongbokgung** to the west and **Changdeokgung** to the east, and thousands of people still call it home. After years of complaints about noise and crowds, Seoul introduced tourist visiting hours here — and since **March 2025** they come with real fines. If you only read one section of this guide, make it the one about the rules.

> **Heads up:** The photos in this guide show Bukchon in different seasons and years, not this week. The evening shots show the alleys at times that are now outside tourist visiting hours in the red zone.

## What Bukchon actually is

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/bukchon-hanok-village-guide/01.jpg" alt="A narrow hanok-lined lane sloping downhill toward the modern skyline of central Seoul and N Seoul Tower" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 1 · The classic view: hanok rooftops in the foreground, downtown Seoul behind.</figcaption>
</figure>

*Bukchon* simply means "north village." During the Joseon dynasty this hilly area between the palaces was where aristocrats and high-ranking officials lived. Most of the houses you see today, though, are younger than that: housing developers began dividing the old estates into smaller lots from the 1910s, and the dense pattern of compact hanok packed side by side mostly took shape in the 1930s. In the 1990s many were torn down for low-rise apartment blocks, until a city program in the early 2000s began helping owners restore and keep their hanok.

The result is the largest cluster of traditional houses in central Seoul, spread across Gahoe-dong, Gye-dong, Samcheong-dong and neighboring districts. Some hanok are now guesthouses, craft workshops, galleries and cafés, but most are still private homes.

## The famous alleys and photo spots

The street almost everyone is looking for is **Bukchon-ro 11-gil** (북촌로11길), in Gahoe-dong. Its uphill and downhill slopes, lined on both sides with hanok, are part of the official "Eight Scenic Views of Bukchon," a set of signposted viewpoints around the area. Others include a view over the rooftops toward Changdeokgung, the craft-workshop street in Wonseo-dong and the stone stairway in Samcheong-dong.

Away from the main alley, slow down and look at the details: carved wooden gates, decorative patterns set into the walls, and the way the roof tiles curve up at the corners. On a clear winter day you can also see the granite peaks of Bugaksan and the mountains to the north behind the rooftops — sometimes with snow on them.

If you want to see inside a hanok, look for the places that are open to the public rather than peering through gates: some houses run as museums, craft workshops or hanok guesthouses. **Baek In-je House**, a 1913 mansion in Gahoe-dong, can be seen from outside during the visiting hours, while its interior tours need a booking through Seoul's public service reservation website.

## The visiting hours (read this before you go)

<figure>
  <img src="/images/posts/bukchon-hanok-village-guide/05.jpg" alt="A signboard on a hanok wall showing the area's visiting hours, next to a parked car" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption>Photo 5 · Signs like this one in the alleys show the visiting hours.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **The red zone:** the area around **Bukchon-ro 11-gil** (about 34,000 m², the part behind Jeongdok Library) is designated a special zone where tourist visits are allowed only from **10am to 5pm**, every day.
- **The fine:** since March 1, 2025, tourists who stay in the red zone between 5pm and 10am — strolling, lingering or taking photos — can be fined **₩100,000 (about US$70)**. In practice, staff patrol the alleys around 5pm, and you'll normally get a warning and a request to leave first.
- **Who's exempt:** residents and their relatives, people visiting a business in the zone (a guesthouse or a shop, for example) and anyone simply passing through.
- **Lower Bukchon** — the shopping street between Anguk Station and the Constitutional Court — is not covered by the curfew.
- **Tour buses:** large tour buses are now banned from the main Bukchon roads, so group tours drop off outside the village.

Rules can be adjusted, so if you're visiting much later than this guide's date, check Jongno-gu's official tourism site before you go.

## How to get there

- **Subway:** Take **Line 3 to Anguk Station** and use **Exit 2**. Walk straight up Bukchon-ro; Bukchon-ro 11-gil is about a **10–12 minute uphill walk**. Wear shoes with grip — some lanes are steep and can be icy in winter.
- **From Myeongdong:** roughly 20–25 minutes. Ride Line 4 one stop to Chungmuro, then change to Line 3 for Anguk.
- **From Hongdae:** roughly 35–40 minutes. Take Line 2 from Hongik University Station to Euljiro 3-ga, then change to Line 3 for Anguk.

Search **북촌한옥마을** or **북촌로11길** in **Naver Map** or **KakaoMap**. Google Maps shows subway and bus routes in Korea, but it doesn't give turn-by-turn walking directions here, and Bukchon's alleys branch off in every direction.

## Tips for international visitors

- **Go in the morning.** Arrive right around 10am on a weekday and you'll have the alleys far more to yourself. Weekend afternoons can be shoulder-to-shoulder.
- **Be a good guest.** Keep your voice down, don't sit on doorsteps or lean against walls for photos, don't open or step through gates, and take your trash and cigarettes elsewhere. Volunteers sometimes hold "please be quiet" signs — they mean it.
- **Money:** walking around is free and you don't need a reservation or a Korean phone number. Cafés and shops around Anguk generally take cards, but keep a backup card handy.
- **Dress for hills and wind.** You'll be outdoors the whole time on exposed slopes, so layer up and bring gloves in December and January.
- **Hanbok rental:** there are rental shops along Bukchon-ro if you want to wear Korean traditional dress for photos.
- **Make a half-day of it:** walk west over to **Samcheong-dong** for cafés and galleries, visit **Changdeokgung** or **Gyeongbokgung** palace, or head south from Anguk Station into **Insadong** for tea houses and craft shops. Hungry afterward? [Gwangjang Market](/posts/gwangjang-market-food-guide/) is a short subway ride away, and from mid-December the [Seoul Lantern Festival](/posts/seoul-lantern-festival-2026/) lights up Cheonggyecheon in the evenings — a good plan for after the 5pm curfew.

## Is it worth it?

Yes — as long as you treat it as a neighborhood walk rather than an attraction. Give it one to two hours, go early, and pair it with a palace or Samcheong-dong to make a full morning. Follow the 10am–5pm window and the quiet-voice rule, and Bukchon is still one of the most beautiful free walks in Seoul.
